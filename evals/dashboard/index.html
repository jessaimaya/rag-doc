Evaluation of a Spanish-language insurance paperwork assistant

Does it find the right paragraph, and does it say so when it can't?

A retrieval-augmented assistant answers patients' questions about IMSS, ISSSTE and private-insurer procedures in Mexico. Here is how six retrieval setups were compared, how the answers were judged, and what the tests changed in the code. Each chart names its source file, and the question explorer shows every query and answer.

Which retrieval technique finds the right passage?

Every question names the document and the phrases a relevant passage must contain, so the judgement does not depend on how the text was split. Hit@1 is the share of questions whose first result was relevant, Hit@5 whether one appears in the top five, and MRR rewards a higher rank (1 is perfect).

Reranking is the big win

A cross-encoder reads the question and each candidate together. It fixed most of the cases where the right passage was found but ranked low, for a few hundred milliseconds.

BM25 added nothing once reranking was on

On this corpus, hybrid and vector-only give the same reranked result. BM25 alone is the weakest setup, partly because the judgement counts only the exact definition passage and BM25 prefers term-dense ones.

Without a reranker, fusion hurt

Merging a weaker ranker with equal weight pulled good vector results down. The weight sweep below shows how far a bias toward vector search recovers.

Can the score tell when there is no answer?

After reranking, the best passage has a relevance score. If it is under a cutoff, the assistant is told "nothing relevant" and should say it doesn't know, instead of improvising. Each dot is one question's best score.

Source: Per-question results. Answerable and out-of-scope scores come from the retrieval eval, near-miss scores from the agent runs.

Unrelated questions are separable. Near misses are not.

Questions about passports or recipes score well below real answers, so the cutoff catches them. Near misses, on topic but absent from the documents, score as high as genuine answers, so retrieval alone cannot reject them. For those the assistant relies on its instructions to admit the gap, and the answer judge checks that it does.

Are the answers right, faithful, and honest about gaps?

Each question went through the real assistant, then an LLM judge checked the answer against the passage it should have used and listed every factual claim with whether the retrieved text supports it. Fabricated page numbers and links were counted with a plain text check.

What the tests changed in the code

  • Codes lost a digitThe retrieval eval's exact-code questions showed that "ST-7" was indexed as "st": the hyphenated number was dropped. Codes now also produce one joined token.
  • Legal articles carried the wrong labelWhile writing questions from the chunks, one labelled "Article 113" turned out to contain Article 112 too. Short neighbouring articles are now labelled as a range, so citations point at the right article.
  • A table of contents read as headingsThe insurer's contents page produced nonsense section names. Lines ending in a page number are no longer headings.
  • The chat model no longer existedRunning the assistant end to end returned a 404 for the old model, then a rejection of the temperature setting on the new one. Both are fixed.
  • The judge could not see page numbersIt marked correct citations as unsupported. It now sees the same page and link metadata the assistant was given.
  • The citation checker misread a postal code"C.P. 00000" in the fictional clinic's address counted as a fabricated page. The pattern is stricter now.
  • The assistant pads answersMany answers add a plausible detail the passages do not state. Mostly harmless, sometimes wrong. A stricter grounding instruction is the next experiment.
  • One real quality gap remainsAsked whether the clinic can issue an IMSS disability note, the assistant answers from the IMSS procedure instead of saying plainly that the clinic is private and does not serve IMSS patients.

Method and limits

How relevance was judged

  • A passage is relevant if it comes from the right document and contains every required phrase, ignoring accents and case.
  • The dataset is checked against the index before every run, so a question without a relevant passage fails loudly.
  • Out-of-scope questions are unrelated (a recipe, a passport). Near misses are on topic, with the answer verified absent by search.

Limits

  • The questions are few and were written after reading the documents, so lexical overlap is probably higher than for real users.
  • Phrase-based judging undercounts valid alternative passages, which penalises BM25 most.
  • One sample per question and one judge. Differences of a question or two are noise.
  • Fictional clinic data only; no patient data was used.
